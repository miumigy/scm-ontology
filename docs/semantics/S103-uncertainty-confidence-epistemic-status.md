# S103 — Uncertainty, Confidence & Epistemic Status Semantics

S103 defines how the ontology represents what is known, observed, estimated, inferred, forecast, simulated, hypothesized, or otherwise uncertain without collapsing fundamentally different forms of uncertainty into a single confidence value.

## Canonical decision

A value or assertion should be interpreted together with its provenance, epistemic status, method, and relevant uncertainty.

```text
Value / Assertion
      +
Provenance
      +
Epistemic Status
      +
Uncertainty
      +
Method
      +
Confidence (when meaningful)
```

These are distinct semantic dimensions.

## Epistemic status

Epistemic Status describes how an assertion or value is known or established.

A reference vocabulary includes:

```text
observed
measured
recorded
estimated
inferred
forecast
projected
simulated
hypothesized
claimed
assumed
unknown
uncertain
```

This vocabulary is extensible and domain-specific statuses may be added without changing the meaning of the canonical Observation primitive.

## Observed

`Observed` indicates that a phenomenon was directly represented as an observation under the applicable Observation semantics.

Observed does not necessarily mean error-free or certain.

```text
Observed value = 512
Measurement uncertainty = ±3
```

The observation can be real while its numerical precision remains limited.

## Measured

`Measured` indicates that a value was obtained through a measurement method or instrument.

A measured value should retain relevant measurement provenance and uncertainty where available.

```text
sensor → measurement → value
```

Not every Observation is necessarily a physical measurement; some observations may be recorded events or reported facts.

## Recorded

`Recorded` indicates that information was captured by a system or actor.

Recording and observation should not be conflated:

```text
phenomenon
   ↓
observation
   ↓
recording
```

A recorded value may contain transcription, timing, or source limitations.

## Estimated

`Estimated` indicates that a value was derived using an estimation method rather than directly observed as that exact value.

```text
Observed inputs
   ↓ estimation method
Estimated value
```

An estimate should not silently become an Observed fact.

## Inferred

`Inferred` indicates that an assertion was derived from available evidence or observations through reasoning or a model.

Inference is distinct from direct observation.

```text
Observation / Evidence
       ↓
    inference
       ↓
 Inferred assertion
```

## Forecast

`Forecast` represents an estimate about a future or currently unknown state generated by a defined forecasting method.

A forecast is not a historical Observation.

```text
Forecast demand = 1,050
```

remains forecast information even if it later turns out to equal the actual demand.

## Projection

`Projected` indicates a modeled continuation or expected trajectory under specified assumptions.

Projection and Forecast may overlap in implementations, but the ontology preserves them as distinguishable semantic concepts where method or assumption provenance matters.

## Simulated

`Simulated` indicates a result generated by a simulation or computational model rather than observed execution.

```text
Simulation result = inventory 480
```

is not equivalent to:

```text
Actual Observation = inventory 480
```

## Hypothesized

`Hypothesized` indicates a proposition proposed for investigation but not yet sufficiently established.

For example:

```text
Supplier delay caused stockout
```

may begin as a hypothesis before causal analysis validates or rejects it.

## Claimed

`Claimed` indicates that an actor or source asserts a proposition.

A claim records the assertion and its source; it does not automatically establish truth.

## Assumed

`Assumed` indicates that a condition is intentionally adopted as an input to reasoning, planning, or scenario analysis.

An assumption can be operationally useful without being a statement about historical reality.

## Unknown

`Unknown` indicates that the relevant value, state, or proposition is not known under the applicable context.

Unknown is not equivalent to zero, null-as-zero, false, or uncertain.

```text
Unknown demand
  ≠
demand = 0
```

## Uncertain

`Uncertain` indicates that multiple possible values, states, or interpretations remain plausible under the available information.

Uncertainty therefore differs from ignorance:

```text
Unknown
  = insufficiently known

Uncertain
  = multiple plausible possibilities / bounded lack of certainty
```

A domain may use a more precise uncertainty model where needed.

## Uncertainty

Uncertainty describes limitations in knowledge about a value, state, event, parameter, or conclusion.

It may arise from:

```text
measurement
sampling
coverage
missing data
model assumptions
forecast error
causal ambiguity
data quality
source disagreement
future variability
```

Different uncertainty sources should not be collapsed unless the applicable method explicitly combines them.

## Measurement uncertainty

Measurement uncertainty concerns the uncertainty associated with obtaining a measured value.

```text
Measurement = 100
Uncertainty = ±2
```

This is distinct from business tolerance and causal uncertainty.

```text
Measurement uncertainty
    ≠ Business tolerance
    ≠ Causal uncertainty
```

## Statistical uncertainty

Statistical uncertainty concerns uncertainty arising from sampling, estimation, stochastic variation, or statistical inference.

It may be represented through distributions, intervals, standard errors, confidence intervals, prediction intervals, or other domain-appropriate measures.

S103 does not mandate a single statistical representation.

## Model uncertainty

Model uncertainty arises when alternative models, assumptions, structures, or parameterizations could produce different results.

```text
Same inputs
 + Model A → result A
 + Model B → result B
```

A model result should therefore retain model provenance when reproducibility matters.

## Forecast uncertainty

Forecast uncertainty describes uncertainty about future values or states.

A forecast point estimate should not be interpreted as certainty merely because it is represented by a single number.

```text
Forecast = 1,050
Forecast interval = [980, 1,130]
```

The interval and the point estimate answer different questions.

## Causal uncertainty

Causal uncertainty concerns uncertainty about whether and how one factor contributed to an outcome.

```text
Action A
   ↓ ?
Outcome O
```

The existence of an observed association does not establish causality.

S101 causal semantics remain applicable.

## Epistemic uncertainty versus aleatory variability

Where the domain distinguishes them:

```text
Epistemic uncertainty
  = uncertainty due to incomplete knowledge

Aleatory variability
  = inherent variation in the phenomenon
```

These may require different analytical treatments and should not be treated as identical merely because both appear as uncertainty.

## Confidence

Confidence expresses the degree of support, trust, or certainty assigned to an assertion under a defined method or interpretation.

Confidence is contextual and should not be treated as a universal probability of truth unless the method explicitly gives it that meaning.

```text
confidence = 0.9
```

is therefore incomplete without knowing what the 0.9 means.

## Confidence versus probability

Probability may represent uncertainty about a random event or parameter under a formal probabilistic model.

Confidence may instead represent a methodological or evaluative judgment.

Therefore:

```text
confidence
  ≠ automatically probability
```

Implementations should preserve the semantics of the scale used.

## Confidence versus accuracy

Confidence in a value or assertion does not guarantee that it is accurate.

```text
high confidence
  ≠ guaranteed correctness
```

Accuracy concerns correspondence with the relevant reality or reference, while confidence concerns the degree of support or trust assigned under a method.

## Confidence versus data quality

Data quality is multidimensional and may include:

```text
completeness
accuracy
timeliness
consistency
validity
uniqueness
lineage
```

Confidence may depend on data quality but is not equivalent to it.

## Source provenance

Epistemic interpretation should preserve source provenance where material.

Examples:

```text
ERP transaction
IoT sensor
planner input
supplier report
forecast model
simulation model
LLM inference
```

Two values with the same numerical value may have different epistemic statuses because their sources and methods differ.

## Method provenance

Derived information should identify the method used when reproducibility or interpretation matters.

```text
Value
 ↓
Method
 ↓
Result
```

Examples include:

```text
forecasting model
optimization solver
statistical estimator
simulation engine
causal analysis
human assessment
LLM inference
```

## Time of knowledge

The time at which information became available or was inferred can differ from the time to which it refers.

```text
reference_time
observed_at
recorded_at
inferred_at
forecast_created_at
```

This distinction is important for avoiding hindsight bias in decision analysis.

## Hindsight boundary

An evaluation of a historical Decision should, where auditability matters, preserve the information and epistemic status available at decision time.

```text
Decision at T1
  uses knowledge available at T1

Later information at T2
  must not silently appear as if known at T1
```

This is especially important for AI-assisted decision evaluation.

## Epistemic status of AI-generated information

AI-generated information should not automatically inherit the epistemic status of its inputs.

For example:

```text
Observed shipment delay
        ↓
LLM inference:
  likely caused by congestion
```

The first is observed evidence; the second is an inferred hypothesis or claim unless independently validated.

## Aggregation and epistemic status

Aggregating Observations does not necessarily preserve the same epistemic status without qualification.

```text
Observed inputs
   ↓ aggregation
Derived metric
```

The derived metric should preserve its derivation method and provenance.

## Scenario and epistemic status

S102 scenario results are hypothetical or modeled unless and until corresponding real-world conditions are observed.

```text
Scenario result
  = modeled / hypothetical

Actual Observation
  = observed / recorded reality
```

A scenario result must not silently become an Observation merely because it is later reused operationally.

## Claims and validation

A Claim may transition in epistemic status as evidence accumulates.

Conceptually:

```text
Hypothesis
   ↓ evidence
Supported Claim
   ↓ validation
Confirmed / accepted conclusion
```

The exact vocabulary is domain-specific.

Historical provenance of earlier statuses should remain available when auditability matters.

## Unknown versus missing

`Missing` describes absence of a data value or record.

`Unknown` describes the epistemic state of the underlying fact.

```text
Missing data
  may result in
Unknown value
```

but they are not synonyms.

## Not applicable

`Not applicable` indicates that a value or concept does not apply to the entity, event, or context.

```text
Unknown
  ≠ Missing
  ≠ Not applicable
  ≠ Zero
```

These distinctions should be preserved where they affect decisions or analysis.

## Composite assertions

A single record may contain components with different epistemic statuses.

```text
Demand = 1,050
  ├─ baseline = forecast
  ├─ promotion effect = estimated
  └─ actual orders = observed
```

The ontology should avoid forcing all components into one global confidence label when their semantics differ.

## No universal confidence field

S103 does not mandate:

```text
confidence: 0..1
```

as a universal property of all entities.

Where confidence is used, its scale, interpretation, method, and provenance should be explicit.

## No epistemic mutation of historical Observation

Later analysis may change the interpretation or confidence assigned to an assertion without changing the original historical Observation itself.

```text
Observation O1
   ↓ later analysis
confidence / interpretation updated

O1 historical content remains intact
```

The ontology should distinguish the original observation from subsequent assessments about it.

## No mandatory epistemic fields on Observation

S103 does not require the canonical Observation primitive to contain all of:

```text
epistemic_status
confidence
uncertainty
method
source
accuracy
probability
```

These belong to appropriate provenance, measurement, assessment, claim, uncertainty, or derived-value models and may be linked to an Observation as needed.

## Non-goals

S103 does not define a universal confidence scoring system, probability calibration method, statistical uncertainty framework, data-quality standard, AI trust score, epistemic logic, or ontology-wide truth-maintenance system.
